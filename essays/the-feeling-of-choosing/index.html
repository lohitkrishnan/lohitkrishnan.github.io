The feeling of choosing

16 November 2026 · 5 min read
Narrated with an AI voice from elevenlabs.io

I no longer believe in free will, and I have never felt more free.

Both halves of that sentence are true, and they refuse to cancel each other out. Reading Robert Sapolsky’s Determined convinced me of the first half. Living my ordinary life keeps insisting on the second. This essay is my attempt to hold the two together without pretending either one away.

The argument I can’t get around

Sapolsky’s case is simple to state and very hard to escape. Every decision I make comes out of my brain as it is in that moment. That state was shaped by what happened seconds earlier, which was shaped by my hormones that morning, my sleep last night, my childhood, my genes, the culture my ancestors built. At no point in that chain can I find a link that I authored from outside the chain.

The brain is made of neurons, neurons are made of molecules, and molecules do what physics tells them to. Physics does not pause to ask my opinion. For free will to exist, something in me would have to reach into that machinery and push it in a direction the prior causes weren’t already taking it. I can’t see what that something would be, or what it would be made of.

Some people look to quantum randomness for an exit. But a decision that turns on a random event is no more mine than one that turns on a determined event. A dice roll inside my skull is still not me choosing.

So I accept the conclusion. If the universe runs on its own, without a little uncaused chooser hidden inside each of us, then what I call my choices are things that happen in me, not things I originate.

And yet, the feeling

None of this touches how it feels to be me on a Tuesday afternoon.

When I think something through slowly, weigh the options, and pick one, I feel a quiet satisfaction. It feels like authorship. I considered, I judged, I decided. That is exactly what free will is supposed to feel like, and the feeling shows up right on schedule.

The stranger case is the opposite one. When I act on impulse, with no weighing at all, I don’t feel like a puppet. I feel more alive than at any other time, and more in control, not less. The snap decision, the yes that comes out before I’ve finished thinking, the sudden swerve in a plan: these feel like the purest expressions of who I am.

That should be backwards. An impulse is the clearest example of my biology acting before “I” get a say. It is neurotransmitters firing faster than reflection can follow. If any of my actions should feel unfree, it is these. Instead they feel like freedom itself.

So I get the feeling of free will at both ends. Deliberation gives me the feeling of being the author. Impulse gives me the feeling of being fully alive. Both are the same machinery, running at different speeds, and both come with the same stamp attached: I did this.

What the illusion is for

Why would a determined creature be built to feel undetermined? My guess is that the feeling does a job.

Imagine a mind that experienced itself accurately, as a place where causes pass through. It might simply let go. Why plan, why resist the easy thing, why apologise, if nothing is up to you? A creature that surrendered to nature that completely would not last long, and would not be much use to the creatures around it.

The feeling of agency prevents that surrender. Because I feel like the author of my actions, I rehearse them in advance, regret them afterwards, and adjust. Because I feel responsible, I can be held to a promise, and I can hold others to theirs. The sense of free will is the handle by which consequences get a grip on behaviour. It is itself one of the causes, and a powerful one.

This is where I part ways a little with Sapolsky. He follows the argument to its end and concludes that blame and praise make no sense. I accept the logic. But I notice that the feeling of responsibility is doing real work in me, and I suspect a life or a society without it would run worse, not better. The illusion may be false and still be load-bearing.

Knowing changes nothing

Here is the part I find most exciting. You would expect that once you see through an illusion, it weakens. This one doesn’t.

It behaves like those optical illusions where two lines are the same length but one looks longer. You can measure them, know the truth with total certainty, and look again. One still looks longer. The knowledge lives in one part of the brain and the seeing happens in another, and the first has no authority over the second.

Free will is like that. I can finish Sapolsky’s book, agree with every chapter, close it, and then decide what to have for dinner. The deciding feels exactly as free as it did before. My belief changed. My biology did not, because the feeling was never a belief in the first place. It is a product of the same machinery the argument describes.

There is a second twist. Even my being convinced was not a free choice. The book reached a brain already prepared, by temperament and training, to find that kind of argument persuasive. I did not choose to agree. Agreement happened. And knowing that doesn’t loosen anything either.

Living inside it

So where does that leave me? In a position I find oddly comfortable.

I think I am a physical process, start to finish. I also think I will go on feeling like a chooser until the day I die, and that no amount of reading will change this. I have stopped treating those two facts as a contradiction to be resolved. One is a claim about how the world works. The other is a report of what it is like to be this particular piece of the world.

What the knowledge does change is small but real. I am slower to judge other people, because I can see more of the chain behind what they do. I am a little gentler with myself for the same reason. And I am more curious about my own impulses, which now look less like lapses of control and more like the machinery showing itself plainly.

I will keep deliberating, and keep feeling satisfied when I do. I will keep acting on impulse, and keep feeling alive when I do. Neither was ever up to me. Both will go on feeling like mine.
